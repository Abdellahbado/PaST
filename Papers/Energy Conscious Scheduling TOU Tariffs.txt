This paper investigates an energy-conscious unrelated parallel machine scheduling problem under timeof-use (TOU) electricity pricing scheme, in which the electricity price varies throughout a day. The
problem lies in assigning a group of jobs to a set of unrelated parallel machines and then scheduling jobs
on each separate machine so as to minimize the total electricity cost. We ﬁrst build an improved
continuous-time mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model for the problem. To tackle large-size
problems, we then propose a two-stage heuristic. Speciﬁcally, at the ﬁrst stage, jobs are assigned to
machines aiming at minimizing the total electricity cost under the preemptive circumstance. At the
second stage, the jobs assigned to each machine are scheduled using an insertion heuristic. Computational results on a real-life instance for turning process and random test instances demonstrate that the
proposed MILP approach is able to solve small-size problems while the two-stage heuristic is appropriate
for large-size problems. The case study for turning process also reveals that the proposed optimization
approaches can contribute to cleaner production.

1. Introduction
The rapid development of global economy has been demanding
more energy and the shortage of energy is threatening the overall
development of many countries. Energy saving has attracted huge
attention in recent years due to the fact that a large proportion of
resources for energy generation are nonrenewable (Tacconi, 2000)
and that energy consumption has grown by as much as 300% over
the last 50 years (Park et al., 2009). Inappropriate usage of energy
has also brought about the serious problem of excessive emissions
of greenhouse gases, which takes a heavy toll on the climate as well
as the environment. Hence, efﬁcient utilization of energy is
important in terms of both promoting economic growth and protecting environment.
It is well known that the industrial sector is the backbone of
many countries and consumes the most energy (Jovane et al., 2008;
Lu et al., 2017). For example, in China, according to Liu (2014), the
industrial sector consumes about half of the total electricity
generated. Therefore, improving the energy efﬁciency in the industrial sector is of great signiﬁcance (Li et al., 2016; Cai et al., 2016;

ElMaraghy et al., 2016). Electricity is the main form of energy utilized by this sector (Sun and Li, 2013), which is transferred to its
customers immediately after being generated. Since the customers'
demand for electricity can vary during one day, the problem of how
to balance the electricity supply and demand can be quite tricky. To
tackle this problem, electricity suppliers usually introduce backup
power facilities (Wang and Li, 2013). However, these facilities are
kept idle for most of the time and cost considerable extra resources.
Another strategy to balance the electricity supply and demand is
through demand-side management programs, also known as demand response (DR) technology (Albadi and El-Saadany, 2008).
The so-called demand-side management programs include a
wide variety of electricity load control strategies and mechanisms,
among which the time-of-use (TOU) pricing policy is the most
prevalent one. This scheme is motivated by the observation that the
total electricity demand in the grid can vary signiﬁcantly in a single
day. As a result, it is recommended that electricity suppliers should
set different prices for electricity consumed in different periods of a
day. Generally, the electricity consumed when the grid is overloaded tends to be set at higher prices while that consumed when
the load is small at lower prices. For example, the TOU pricing
scheme executed in Shaanxi Province, China, consists of six pricing
periods for a whole day and the electricity price during on-peak
periods is almost triple of that during off-peak periods (Che et al.,

2016). This implies that if some workloads are shifted from on-peak
periods to off-peak or mid-peak periods, signiﬁcant electricity cost
may be saved. Such schemes enable us to utilize the current infrastructures more efﬁciently (Albadi and El-Saadany, 2008) and
improve the grid's carbon efﬁciency as well (Wang and Li, 2013).
Scheduling focuses on how to allocate limited resources to
various tasks optimally (Pinedo, 2012). Recently, driven by the
pressure of energy saving and environment protection, scheduling
problems under the TOU electricity pricing scheme have been
studied by several scholars. For example, Zhang et al. (2014)
examined a ﬂow shop scheduling problem with the objective of
minimizing both the electricity cost and the carbon footprint and
formulated a multi-objective mixed-integer linear programming
(MILP) model for their problem. It was shown that there is a tradeoff between the two objectives. Luo et al. (2013) addressed a hybrid
ﬂow shop scheduling problem considering both production and
energy efﬁciency. The aim was to minimize both the makespan and
the electricity cost and an ant colony optimization meta-heuristic
was proposed to solve their problem. Sharma et al. (2015) investigated a multi-machine scheduling problem with variable processing speeds to minimize the electricity cost and environmental
impact and developed a simulated annealing algorithm. Shrouf
et al. (2014) studied a single-machine scheduling problem taking
into account the change of machine mode. They developed a genetic algorithm to obtain near-optimal solutions for the problem
and revealed that shifting the workload from on-peak periods to
off-peak periods can reduce electricity cost signiﬁcantly. Fang et al.
(2016) examined single-machine scheduling problems with ﬁxed
and variable processing speeds to minimize the total electricity
cost. Che et al. (2016) proposed a continuous-time MILP model and
an efﬁcient greedy insertion heuristic algorithm for the the singlemachine scheduling problem with ﬁxed processing speed. Wang
et al. (2016b) investigated a single-machine batch scheduling
problem with variable processing speeds and proposed a discretetime MILP model and two heuristics based on the MILP model.
Parallel machine scheduling problem is of great signiﬁcance due
to the fact that it is not only a generalization of the single-machine
scheduling problem, but also a special case of the ﬂexible ﬂow shop
scheduling problem (Pinedo, 2012). Parallel machine scheduling
problems usually involve a two-stage decision-making process:
assigning jobs to machines and then sequencing the assigned jobs
on each machine. Traditionally, the majority of such problems are
aimed to optimize the criteria pertaining to production efﬁciency,
such as makespan (e.g., Rustogi and Strusevich, 2013) and tardiness
(e.g., Lin and Lin, 2015). For parallel machine scheduling under the
TOU pricing scheme, Moon et al. (2013) investigated an unrelated
parallel machine scheduling problem to minimize the weighted
sum of makespan and electricity costs. They proposed a hybrid
genetic algorithm with a blank job insertion algorithm for the
problem, which is able to solve problems with up to 65 jobs and 20
machines. Ding et al. (2016) addressed a similar problem to minimize the total electricity cost. They ﬁrst formulated a time-intervalbased MILP model for the problem and then proposed a column
generation heuristic. Their approaches can deal with problems with
up to 200 jobs and 20 machines. Zeng et al. (2017) proposed an
insertion heuristic and an iterative search framework for the biobjective uniform parallel machine scheduling problem.

In this paper, we address an energy-conscious unrelated parallel
machine scheduling problem under the TOU tariffs with the
objective of minimizing the total electricity cost. To tackle such a
problem, we ﬁrst formulate an improved MILP model to obtain
optimal solutions for small-size problems. We then propose an
efﬁcient two-stage heuristic to deal with large-size problems. The
ﬁrst stage focuses on assigning jobs to machines aiming at minimizing the total electricity cost under the preemptive circumstance. That is, jobs are allowed to be preemptively processed on a
machine. The second stage is related to scheduling jobs on each
machine to minimize the total electricity cost without preemption.
Computational results show the proposed optimization approaches
can contribute to cleaner production; the proposed MILP approach
is able to solve small-size problems while the two-stage heuristic is
appropriate for large-size problems.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2,
we present a detailed description for the problem considered and
formulate an improved MILP model. An efﬁcient two-stage heuristic is proposed in Section 3. We present the computational results in Section 4 and draw the conclusions in Section 5.

2. MILP formulation for the problem
2.1. Problem description and basic model
In the considered problem, there are N jobs to be processed on M
parallel machines. Each job, say job i, is characterized by its processing time on machine j, ti,j, and power consumption per unit
time, called electricity consumption rate, on machine j, pi,j,
1  i  N, 1  j  M. Note that the processing time and the power
consumption rate of a job are dependent on both the machine and
the job itself. Thus, the problem under investigation is an unrelated
parallel machine scheduling problem. Each machine can process
only one job at a time and each job cannot be processed on more
than one machine at the same time. No preemption is allowed. All
jobs and machines are available at the beginning of the production
horizon. Furthermore, machines breakdown and their preventive
maintenance are not considered in this paper.
As mentioned above, the electricity prices follow a TOU pricing
scheme. That is to say, the prices of electricity, depending on its
demands, may vary throughout a day. In a TOU pricing scheme, the
time horizon is divided into a set of K time periods. Period k,
1  k  K, is featured by its start time sk and electricity price ck,
1  k  K. Without loss of generality, we set s1 ¼ 0. Period k can be
represented by the interval [sk, skþ1], 1  k  K. The duration of
period k is denoted by Tk (Tk ¼ skþ1  sk), 1  k  K. Let sKþ1 be the
given makespan (maximum completion time) for processing all
jobs.
The problem considered in this paper consists in assigning the N
jobs to the M unrelated parallel machines and then allocating
available time periods on each machine to the jobs assigned to it so
as to minimize the total electricity cost. Note that a job may be
processed across two or more adjacent periods on a machine. We
choose the following decision variables to formulate this problem.

Theorem 2. In the optimal solution of model P1, for any machine j
and period k, if multiple jobs with the same electricity consumption
rate are processed across periods k and kþ1 of machine j, we can
always obtain a non-preemptive schedule with the same total
electricity cost as TEC * .

jobs i and l are both processed across periods k and k þ1 of machine
j and pi,j ¼ pl,j. By assumption, we have x*i;j;k > 0, x*i;j;kþ1 > 0, x*l;j;k > 0,
and x*l;j;kþ1 > 0. In this case, it is clear that at least one job is processed in a preemptive way. Similarly, we can move all other jobs
processed in period k of machine j within the period such that the
processing of jobs i and l in period k happens at the end of period k;
and move all other jobs processed in period kþ1 of machine j
within the period such that the processing of jobs i and l in period
kþ1 happens at the start of period kþ1. Now we can insert jobs i
and l in an arbitrary order into the segment across period k and kþ1
with the duration x*i;j;k þ x*l;j;k þ x*i;j;kþ1 þ x*l;j;kþ1 . Note that after
insertion, the duration in period k is still equal to x*i;j;k þ x*l;j;k and
that in period kþ1 is still x*i;j;kþ1 þ x*l;j;kþ1 . Since pi,j ¼ pl,j, it is clear
that the electricity cost required for handling jobs i and l remains
the same. Note that the electricity cost required for processing
other jobs remains unchanged. For this reason, the total electricity
cost is still equal to TEC * . If more than two jobs with the same
electricity consumption rate are processed across periods k and
k þ1 of machine j, we can always obtain a non-preemptive schedule
with the same total electricity cost in a similar way. This ends the
proof.

According to Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, it is understandable
that the optimal schedule obtained with model P1 may be a preemptive one. However, if this is so, we can always construct a nonpreemptive schedule with the same total electricity cost from the
preemptive schedule. Thus, we have the following corollary, which
demonstrates the correctness of model P1.
Corollary 1. Constraints (4) and (5) can be relaxed in model P0.
With Corollary 1, ui,j,k is unnecessary and constraints (4)e(5) can
be removed from model P0 and we thus have model P1, which becomes more compact in terms of the numbers of variables and
constraints.
As mentioned above, Ding et al. (2016) also proposed an MILP
model for the unrelated parallel machine scheduling problem. We
now describe the differences between model P1 and Ding et al.'s
model (Ding-model for short). It should be noted that variables xi,j,k
and yi,j,k are taken from Ding-model and constraints (1)e(3) in
model P1 are equivalent to constraints (11)e(13) in Ding-model.
The difference between the two models lies in the following two
aspects. First, constraints (4) and (5) are relaxed and not necessary
in our model. As such, the binary variable ui,j,k is no longer necessary. This reduces NMK binary variables and NM(K1)þMK constraints. Second, constraint (6) and (7) are used to ensure the
processing continuity of a job in multiple periods. In order to
formulate the processing contiguity constraint, Ding et al. deﬁned
additional 2NMK binary variables and the total number of associated constraints is 3NMK þ N. In this work, we formulate the processing contiguity constraints without deﬁning any additional
variables while the number of associated constraints is 2NM(Ke2).
In addition, we introduce an additional two-dimension binary
variable vi,j and additional constraints (8) and (9) to ensure that
each job can be processed on one and only one machine. Its total
numbers of variables and constraints are NM and NM þ N,
respectively. The valid inequalities (14) and (15) count 2NM constraints. From the above analysis, it is clear that our model is more
compact than Ding-model in terms of the number of variables and
the number of constraints.
3. An efﬁcient two-stage heuristic
As mentioned above, parallel machine scheduling problems can
be decomposed into two decision issues. The ﬁrst one is to assign
the jobs to the machines and the second one is to schedule the jobs
on each machine. In view of this observation, we propose a twostage heuristic to solve our problem. At the ﬁrst stage, we assign
the jobs to machines aiming at minimizing the total electricity cost
under the preemptive circumstance. At the second stage, the jobs
assigned to each machine are inserted one by one into its available
time period(s) with minimum electricity cost without preemption.
The reason why we consider a preemptive schedule at the ﬁrst
stage is explained as follows. We observe that if a preemptive
schedule leads to a lower total electricity cost, then its nonpreemptive version tends to take less electricity cost as well. In
view of this point, the preemptive schedule is considered to save
computational time when assigning the jobs to the machines.
3.1. Assignment of jobs to the machines
The ﬁrst stage of the heuristic focuses on assigning the jobs to
the machines, aiming at minimizing the total electricity cost under
the preemptive circumstance. To achieve this, we ﬁrst determine
the assignment order of jobs and then assign the jobs one after
another to the machines. Inspired by the Vogel's method of allocation for classical transportation problems, we introduce the
concept of penalty cost to determine the assignment order of jobs.

Before presenting the deﬁnition of penalty costs, let us ﬁrst review some problem properties. As mentioned above, the processing time of job i on machine j is ti,j, while its corresponding
electricity consumption rate is pi,j. Let q0i;j denote the total amount of
electricity consumed by machine j for processing job i. By deﬁnition, we have q0i;j ¼ ti,jpi,j. Note that different machines may require
different amounts of electricity consumption to process the same
job. That is, each job may require different amounts of electricity
consumption on different machines. Note that the total electricity
cost is dependent on the amount of electricity consumption and its
price. For this reason, it is favorable to assign a job to the machine
with less electricity consumption. However, due to limited capacity
of each machine, not all jobs can be assigned to their respective
machines with the least electricity assumption. To solve this issue,
we calculate the difference between the two least electricity consumptions for each job on the machines. The difference is called the
penalty cost of a job for not being processed on the machine with
the least electricity consumption. It is understandable that the job
with a higher penalty cost should be given a higher priority to be
ﬁrst assigned, called Highest-Penalty-Cost-First for short. To
implement this assignment rule, we ﬁrst sort all jobs in nonincreasing order of their penalty costs and let the order be 4(1),
4(2), …, 4(N), where 4(i) is the index of the i-th job. We then assign
the jobs to the machines one after another according to this order.
That is, we ﬁrst assign job 4(1) among the machines, then job 4(2),
and ﬁnally job 4(N). Note that if several jobs share the same value of
penalty cost, then the job with lower least electricity consumption
is granted higher priority to be ﬁrst assigned.
Once the job assignment order is determined, we need to decide
to which machine each job should be assigned. Thus, our problem
becomes how to assign a given job to a machine. Suppose that we
need to determine to which machine job i should be assigned at
some moment. Let Sj(i) be the set of jobs that have been assigned to
machine j before assigning job i. And let Cj(i) denote the optimal
total electricity cost for processing all the jobs in set Sj(i) on machine j in a preemptive way. With preemption, it means that jobs
can be preemptively processed on machine j. That is, the processing
of a job can be paused and resumed at a later time point. According
to Fang et al. (2016), the optimal preemptive schedule for scheduling multiple jobs on a single machine under TOU tariffs to
minimize the total electricity cost can be obtained in the following
way. First, all periods are sorted in non-decreasing order of their
prices and let the order be f(1), f(2), …, f(K) such that
cf(1)  cf(2)  …  cf(K), where f(k) is the index of the k-th period.
The jobs are then inserted one by one, in non-increasing of their
electricity consumption rates, into the sorted periods f(1), f(2), …,
f(K). That is, the ﬁrst job is inserted into period f(1) and then the
other jobs are inserted into this period until it is fully occupied.
When period f(1) is fully occupied, the remaining jobs are inserted
into period f(2) and this process continues until all the jobs have
been inserted. Note that due to preemption, part of a job may be
processed in period f(k) and its remaining part processed in period
f(k þ 1) for some 1  k  Ke1.

denoted by Ai,j, as Ai;j ¼ Cj0 ðiÞ  Cj ðiÞ. That is, the assignment cost of

after another to the machines and the process ends when all the

3.2. Insertion heuristic to schedule jobs on a single machine

4. Computational results

After the ﬁrst stage of the heuristic, each machine is allocated
with certain number of jobs. Hence, the second stage is to schedule
the jobs on each single machine separately in a non-preemptive
way with the objective of minimizing the total electricity cost.
Thus, at the second stage, the problem can be transformed into a
series of single-machine scheduling problems under TOU tariffs.
This problem has been studied by Che et al. (2016), who proposed
an efﬁcient greedy insertion heuristic to minimize the total electricity cost. Hence, in this paper, we utilize their algorithm to
schedule the jobs on each machine in order to minimize the total
electricity cost.
Generally, the greedy insertion heuristic proposed by Che et al.
(2016) tries to insert the jobs one by one into the position that
brings about the least electricity cost. The jobs are inserted in nonincreasing order of their electricity consumption rates. In their
work, they proposed the concept of block to deﬁne the candidate
insertion positions for jobs, which are based on the fact that the
movement of a job incurs no change to the total electricity cost as
long as its start time and completion time are always within the
same period. Actually, each period on a machine provides two
blocks for jobs to insert: the forward block and the backward
blocks. The forward and backward directions refer to the directions
along which time increases and decreases, respectively. The forward (resp. backward) block from a certain period on a machine is
deﬁned as the possible longest idle segment from this period to its
ﬁrst non-empty forward (resp. backward) period considering the
movement of jobs within a period.
Note that as pointed out by Che et al. (2016), the movement of
previously inserted jobs should be taken into account when the
forward and backward blocks are not large enough. This will help to
improve the performance of the algorithm. To achieve this, they

4.1. A real-life instance for turning process
Turning operation is one of the most fundamental machining
processes. A turning process is usually comprised of two stages:
rough turning and ﬁne turning. In this work, a real-life instance for
turning process from Wang et al. (2016a) is used to validate the
proposed MILP model and two-stage heuristic. In this instance, jobs
to be handled have to undergo the rough turning process and then
the ﬁne turning process. At each stage, each job can be processed on
one of unrelated parallel machines. Speciﬁcally, there are in total
eleven C630 lathes involved, ﬁve for the rough turning and six for
the ﬁne turning. Note that each stage of the turning process can be
considered as a separate unrelated parallel machine environment.
For simplicity, this work only considers the ﬁrst stage, i.e., rough
turning.
The jobs to be processed are different in terms of materials,
diameters as well as lengths. There are 12 types of jobs depending
on the above features. As mentioned above, ﬁve lathes are available
for the rough turning process. Each lathe has 12 different spindle
speeds to choose. Different speed levels correspond to different
processing times and amounts of electricity consumption. For the
purpose of energy saving, we select the optimal speed determined
by Wang et al. (2016a) for each job to be processed on a particular
machine. The processing time and its corresponding electricity
consumption for a job processed on a speciﬁc machine can be

very effective. The total amount of electricity consumption tends to

optimization approach contributes to cleaner production. On the

can also contribute to balancing the demand and supply of electricity and consequently reducing CO2 emission (Shrouf et al., 2014;

5. Conclusion
This paper considers an unrelated parallel machine scheduling

A. Che et al. / Journal of Cleaner Production 156 (2017) 688e697

problem under the TOU tariffs to minimize the total electricity cost.
We ﬁrst formulate an improved MILP model for the problem. To
tackle large-size problem, we then develop an efﬁcient two-stage
heuristic.
The proposed MILP approach and two-stage heuristic are ﬁrst
evaluated using a real-life instance for turning process. Computational results demonstrate that the relative gap of the Pareto points
obtained by the heuristic ranges from 0.76% to 1.76%. This suggests
that the two-stage heuristic can yield high-quality Pareto solutions.
Computational experiment also reveals that the total electricity
cost may be saved by prolonging the makespan and the proposed
optimization approaches can contribute to cleaner production.
To further evaluate the performance of our improved MILP
model, we compare it with Ding-model using random test instances. Computational comparison between the two models
shows that our model runs much faster than Ding-model, the ratio
of computational time of Ding-model against our model ranging
from 2.17 to 193.61. This is because our model is more compact in
terms of the number of variables and constraints. We also evaluate
the performance of the heuristic using random test instances. Our
computational results demonstrate that the gap between the total
electricity costs obtained by the heuristic and by the MILP model
ranges from 3.0% to 8.6%. All the instances can be solved within 0.1 s
using the proposed heuristic, while four instances out of 60 instances cannot be solved to optimality within 3600s using the MILP
model. This suggests that the proposed heuristic can yield highquality solution with very short amount of time.
Several management implications can be obtained from this
research when formulating production plans for unrelated parallel
machines under the TOU pricing scheme. Firstly, production managers are suggested to ﬁrst determine the production plan for the
jobs with large gaps between the two least electricity consumptions required for its processing on the machines and each job
should be processed on the machine consuming as less electricity
as possible while balancing the load among the machines. Secondly, jobs should be tried to be processed in the off-peak periods if
the makespan is large enough. Thirdly, there is a trade-off between
the total electricity cost and the makespan. The total electricity cost
can be reduced by appropriately prolonging the makespan.
This work has a close connection with cleaner production from
two points of views. The proposed two-stage heuristic is aimed at
minimizing the total electricity cost under the TOU tariffs. The ﬁrst
stage of the heuristic focuses on minimizing the total amount of
electricity consumption by assigning jobs to machines appropriately. It is understandable that the less the electricity consumption
is, the less the carbon emissions are. The second stage tries to
minimize the total electricity costs on each separate machine when
the total amount of electricity consumption for the jobs is ﬁxed. Our
computational results revealed that the off-peak periods with
lower electricity price has a higher possibility to be used than peak
periods. Thus, the schedule obtained contributes to balancing the
demand and supply of electricity. This may spare the utilization of
backup machines for electricity suppliers, which usually have
higher CO2 emission. Thus, the TOU policy can contribute to the
reduction of CO2 emission and cleaner production.
The TOU pricing policy is now being implemented in many
countries. Under TOU electricity tariffs, the total electricity cost can
be saved by an effective schedule. The proposed MILP model
executed by CPLEX and the heuristic can be used by production
managers to make production plans aiming at reducing total electricity cost under TOU electricity tariffs.
Future research can be focused on the extension of the problem
to other machine environments, such as ﬂow shop and job shop,
and investigating the multi-objective version of the problem using
